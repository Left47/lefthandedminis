## The biggest tip: just get started

You improve with practice. That's it, that's the secret. Too many people wait until they feel "ready," which means they never start. You don't get ready and then paint. You paint, and that's how you get ready.

It also helps to remember that nothing here is permanent. You can always strip a mini and repaint it, so don't be precious about them. I painted a lot of my favorite characters early on, and honestly, they look like crap to me now. I still love them.

## The short version

You don't need ten techniques on mini number one. You need ten chances to get a little better at one or two things. Paint ten minis, give each one a single focus, and photograph every one of them, even the rough ones. A decent photo shows you what to fix next and proves you're improving.

## A roadmap for the first ten

This isn't a rulebook. Swap the order to fit whatever minis you actually have.

1. **Clean basecoats.** Thin paint, two coats, stay inside the lines. That's the whole assignment.
2. **Washes.** Same as mini one, then a wash over everything. Watch how much shading you get for free.
3. **Drybrushing.** Pick a mini with fur, rock, chainmail or rough texture and drybrush the raised edges.
4. **One simple highlight.** Take each main color one step lighter and hit only the top edges.
5. **Color contrast.** Choose a scheme with a clear warm-versus-cool or light-versus-dark split. See [planning the palette](../plan-the-palette/).
6. **Metallics.** Armor, weapons, gadgets. [Drybrushed metallics](../metallic-drybrushing/) are a great first try.
7. **Faces.** Eyes are scary. Do them anyway. A dark line and a dot is enough at tabletop distance.
8. **Basing.** Texture paint, sand or a couple of tufts. It makes a mini look finished faster than anything else.
9. **Photography.** Use the [budget photo setup](../budget-mini-photography/) and compare mini 9 to mini 1. A sharp photo shows thick paint and missed edges you can't see at arm's length.
10. **One advanced idea.** A glow, a blend, a bit of freehand. Just one.

## What my own first steps actually looked like

I didn't follow a tidy list either. Looking back through my posts, the "firsts" came one at a time:

- **First basing materials** on Winter Soldier: some dirt texture and a few grass tufts. Nothing fancy, and it made him pop immediately.
- **First kitbash** on Mysterio, when his box was missing an entire arm.
- **First space marines**, the same month I first built models off the sprue, applied decals and tried an airbrush.
- **First Speedpaints** on Werewolf by Night, as a base layer to move faster.
- **First mostly monochrome mini** on Rhino, which turned into a surprise lesson in values.

Most of those were one new thing on an otherwise normal mini. That's the pattern worth copying.

If I could tell my 2023 self one thing, it would be the same thing at the top of this page: stop waiting, pick up the brush, and let the early ones be rough.

## Tradeoffs

- **One-piece minis vs. kits.** "Paint what you get" minis like Marvel United let you skip assembly and practice painting. Multipart kits teach building but slow the painting reps down.
- **Speed vs. polish.** Early on, finishing matters more. A done mini teaches you more than a half-perfect one.
- **Paint your favorite characters now, or save them?** Now. They'll look rough to you in a year, and you'll still love them. If it really bugs you, strip and repaint.
